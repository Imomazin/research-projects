# Theory draft

## Risk convergence as a strategic problem

Strategic risk research has long treated the external environment as a set of separable conditions. Classic work on task environments described munificence, complexity and dynamism as distinct dimensions (Dess and Beard, 1984), and Milliken (1987) separated uncertainty about the state of the environment from uncertainty about its effects and about the right response. Enterprise risk management inherited this separability. Its frameworks ask firms to list risks, score each on likelihood and impact and aggregate the scores into a portfolio view (Nocco and Stulz, 2006; Hoyt and Liebenberg, 2011; Bromiley et al., 2015). The empirical literature that measures risk from corporate text has largely followed the same pattern, building one measure per category: political risk (Hassan et al., 2019), epidemic exposure (Hassan et al., 2023), climate exposure (Sautner et al., 2023) and cyber risk (Florackis et al., 2023). Systemic-risk scholars argue that this view misses the defining feature of the recent period, in which shocks interact and transmit through shared infrastructure, supply networks and policy responses (Helbing, 2013; Renn et al., 2019). Lawrence et al. (2024) describe a global polycrisis in which crises in different systems entangle and amplify one another, and climate scientists use the term compound events for hazards whose joint impact exceeds the sum of their separate effects (Zscheischler et al., 2018). For a firm the question is narrower and more practical: whether exposure to several emerging risks at once changes what happens to performance when a systemic shock arrives.

The years since 2018 supply unusually clear variation for that question. Trade policy uncertainty reached record levels in 2018 and 2019 and again in 2025, the pandemic closed markets and factories in 2020, global supply-chain pressure peaked in late 2021, and the invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 pushed geopolitical risk to its highest level since the Iraq war while inflation and interest rates rose together (Baker et al., 2016; Caldara and Iacoviello, 2022; Ahir et al., 2022; Benigno et al., 2022). Practitioner commentary in this journal has followed these events closely, from calls to revisit risk management during the pandemic (Sheehan, 2020) and to treat risk as a source of strategy (Calandro, 2020) to work on the preparedness paradox (Day and Dennis, 2022), threat resilience in ecosystems (Dencik et al., 2023), scenario practice (Schoemaker, 2022; Mortlock and Osiyevskyy, 2023), regulatory exposure (Welch, 2026) and frameworks for strategic response to disruption (Soleimani and Stauffer, 2026). Large-sample evidence on how many of these risks firms face at once, and whether that combination matters for performance, remains thin.

## Sensing, interpreting and reconfiguring

Dynamic capabilities theory offers the main explanation for why firms facing the same environment perform differently. Teece et al. (1997) and Teece (2007) describe capacities to sense threats and opportunities, to seize them through investment decisions and to reconfigure assets as conditions change, and later work ties these capacities to managerial cognition and to agility under deep uncertainty (Helfat and Peteraf, 2015; Teece et al., 2016; Schilke et al., 2018). Strategic foresight sharpens the sensing step. Ansoff (1975) argued that firms can respond to weak signals before threats take full form, and longitudinal evidence links mature foresight practice to later profitability and market value (Rohrbeck and Kum, 2018; Vecchiato, 2012). The attention-based view adds that what executives notice and discuss shapes what the organisation does (Ocasio, 1997), and studies of incumbents facing technical change show that attention expressed in corporate text precedes strategic action (Eggers and Kaplan, 2009; Nadkarni and Barr, 2008; Cho and Hambrick, 2006). Organisational ambidexterity explains how firms hold exploitation of current operations alongside exploration of new positions (March, 1991; O'Reilly and Tushman, 2013), which matters when several threats demand simultaneous protection of the core and renewal. These perspectives overlap. In this study dynamic capabilities supply the main mechanism, foresight specifies how sensing operates when signals are numerous and linked and ambidexterity is retained as an explanation for why some responses are affordable. The data cannot measure ambidexterity directly, so it is not tested.

Convergence makes each step harder. When several categories of risk are material at once, executive attention is divided across more domains (Ocasio, 1997), responses compete for the same slack resources (Bourgeois, 1981) and actions that protect against one threat can raise exposure to another, as when a firm concentrates sourcing to reduce cost and so becomes more exposed to trade and geopolitical disruption (Baldwin and Freeman, 2022; Ivanov and Dolgui, 2020). Network studies of supply chains show that shocks travel through linked suppliers and amplify losses beyond the directly affected firm (Carvalho et al., 2021; Elliott et al., 2022; Hendricks and Singhal, 2005). A firm exposed to several emerging risks is therefore more likely to meet a systemic shock on several fronts at once, with less room to redeploy resources between them. Resilience research describes the capacity to absorb such shocks and recover (Williams et al., 2017; Linnenluecke, 2017; Duchek, 2020; Hillmann and Guenther, 2021) and shows that slack, prior practices and financial flexibility shape both the depth of the initial fall and the speed of recovery (Ortiz-de-Mandojana and Bansal, 2016; DesJardine et al., 2019; Fahlenbrach et al., 2021; Ding et al., 2021). Breadth of exposure should therefore deepen the initial fall when a systemic shock hits.

H1. In a systemic shock, firms with greater pre-shock convergence of emerging risk exposures experience greater performance loss, after accounting for the intensity of each risk category.

## Disclosure and documented response

Risk disclosure is an imperfect record of risk management. Since 2005 US registrants have been required to describe their most significant risks in Item 1A of the annual report, and accounting research shows that these disclosures carry information about firm risk that investors use (Campbell et al., 2014; Kravet and Muslu, 2013; Hope et al., 2016). The same research documents growing length, repetition and boilerplate (Dyer et al., 2017; Beatty et al., 2019; Cohen et al., 2020), so that a risk named in Item 1A may describe the hazard without any change in what the firm does. Institutional theory calls the gap between formal statements and practice decoupling (Meyer and Rowan, 1977; Westphal and Zajac, 2001; Bromley and Powell, 2012), and Power (2009) argued that enterprise risk management often produces documentation that reassures rather than prepares. Selective or symbolic disclosure is well documented in environmental reporting (Marquis et al., 2016). Two firms that disclose the same cyber or supply-chain exposure can differ sharply in what they have done about it, and the dynamic capabilities view predicts that the firm which has acted will absorb a shock better (Teece, 2007; Wenzel et al., 2021).

The study therefore separates three things that are often merged: the risks a firm discloses, the actions it reports having taken on those risks and the resources it commits. Response coverage is the share of a firm's material risk categories for which its business description or management discussion reports a completed action. Coverage is still disclosure, but it records actions rather than hazards, and its correspondence with the risk profile indicates whether sensing has turned into seizing and reconfiguring (Teece, 2007; Day and Schoemaker, 2016). Neunsinger et al. (2026) show that pre-crisis disclosure of slack, learning and innovation capabilities in 10-K filings was associated with investor recognition of resilience during the pandemic; the present study shifts attention from capabilities in general to the match between specific exposures and specific actions, and from share prices to operating outcomes.

H2. In a systemic shock, firms with greater pre-shock response coverage experience smaller performance loss and stronger recovery.

H3. Response coverage weakens the association between pre-shock risk convergence and performance loss.
